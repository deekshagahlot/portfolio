Designing the agentic layer for India's largest commerce enablement platform

Shiprocket Agentic

Shiprocket is a technology-enabled ecommerce platform. Indian sellers run their commerce operations on it, shipping, courier selection, exceptions, returns, finance and support, and in FY26 it served 2.14 lakh active merchants across more than 200 million transactions. It listed publicly in August 2026.

Sellers reach all of that through one surface: the seller panel. My work was a full revamp of it, three new agentic surfaces, and a redesign of the panel underneath them, so that a seller moving between the two never feels like they have changed products.

I was the only designer on it. I ran the research that determined what we would build, made the call that changed what the product was, wrote the rules governing what an agent may do with a seller's money, and tested the agents myself until they broke. The CEO announced the result on stage at SHIVIR, our flagship seller event, where I ran the booth demo for over 500 sellers.

The product

A full revamp of Shiprocket's seller panel: three agentic surfaces, chat with an agent per module, proactive insights that carry their own fix, and automations a seller builds by describing them, plus a redesign of the panel underneath, so the two feel like one product.

Why now

The panel was not the problem; the model of use was. Sellers do not lose time because features are missing, but because every feature asks them to know where to go and what to check. Operations outgrew navigation, Shiprocket already holds the data that makes the decisions, and AI moved from summarising to acting.

The decision

I turned down the chat box everyone expected and reframed it: the seller stops operating and starts reviewing. Manual work becomes a conversation, repeated conversations become automations.

Argued as business exposure rather than design preference, a chat box moves the burden of knowing what to ask onto sellers who do not know what to ask.

The rules

Five standards every agent action obeys, written before the second agent existed: one thread many agents; problems come to the seller; state scope in rupees and quantities; give the reason, never a confidence score; separate reading from doing.

Colour had to stop being decoration and start carrying meaning. Brand was demoted to accent, the canvas went neutral, and one deep green is reserved for agentic surfaces alone. Neutral is your workspace, green is the system speaking, red is something wrong.

Honesty

Once a parcel is with a courier the system can diagnose but not rescue, and it says so. A system that overstates its reach once will be checked forever afterwards.

Result

Live beta with ~200 sellers. Daily time on the panel down from 6–7 hours to about 2. 135 of 200 stayed rather than switching back.

The 65 who went back are high-volume sellers with years of muscle memory, a sequencing problem, not a model problem, and the shape of the next release.

This is the short version.

Why now

The panel is not the problem. The model of use is

The existing seller panel is a capable product, and years of work went into making it so. Sellers do not lose time because Shiprocket lacks features. They lose time because every feature still asks them to know where to go, what to check, which exception matters, and what to do about it. That is what a dashboard-led product asks of a person, and it was a reasonable thing to ask when a seller shipped 50 orders a day.

Three shifts made this the right moment to change that model rather than add to it.

  • Seller operations got harder faster than navigation could scale

    More channels, SKUs, courier choices, exception types and reconciliation. A navigation-led product holds at 50 orders a day. At 500 it asks the seller to hire someone.

  • Shiprocket already holds the data that makes the decisions

    Courier performance by route, delivery outcomes, RTO patterns, weight disputes, wallet and COD, support signals. The judgement sellers were making by hand already existed inside the platform, it just had no way to reach them when it mattered.

  • AI moved from summarising to acting

    The opportunity was no longer a box that answers questions but agents that diagnose, recommend and execute inside controlled workflows, a design problem long before it is a model problem.

So the brief I set was not "add AI to the panel". It was: if the platform already knows what a seller should do next, what should the interface be?

The design problem

Why this was hard to design, not just hard to build

  • The consequences are real money, immediately

    An agent that assigns 300 shipments to the wrong courier has spent the seller's money and touched their customers before anyone notices. Undo is not a button here, so the design has to prevent rather than reverse.

  • Sellers cannot independently verify what the system decides

    A seller has no way to check whether a courier recommendation was the right one. That makes shown reasoning a functional requirement rather than a nice-to-have, it is the only verification method available to them.

  • A conversation has no fixed states

    A form has known screens. An agentic surface generates responses nobody will individually design, so the work is writing rules and grammar, how an agent asks, proposes, confirms and admits doubt, that hold for outputs I will never see.

Research

Four streams, run in the first week

Everyone in the company had a theory about what an AI panel should be. I wanted the shape of the product to come from sellers instead, and I treated the overlap between these four streams as the signal worth acting on.

  • Interview calls with sellers

    Conversations across order volumes and categories, from social-commerce sellers to established brands. I did not ask what they wanted from AI, that returns feature requests. I asked them to walk me through yesterday morning, screen by screen, and say where they got stuck, what they abandoned, and what they were guessing at.

  • The KAM team

    They speak to large sellers daily and carry a year of unresolved friction. They surfaced the problems sellers no longer raise, because they have stopped expecting them to be fixed.

  • The AI survey

    Open responses from the wider seller base. Useful less for stated preferences than for the spread of comfort with AI, which told me the product had to work for an outright sceptic on day one.

  • The seller query corpus

    Real support and copilot queries in sellers' own words, behaviour rather than stated preference. It set the scope of each agent and the empty-state prompts.

I ran the same interview set again after beta, with the same sellers where possible, so the before and after are comparable rather than two unrelated snapshots.

The call I owned

Turning down the version the company expected

The assumed answer, inside Shiprocket and across the category, was a chat box in the panel. It was the cheapest thing to build and the easiest thing to announce. The research said it would be politely ignored by exactly the sellers who had the most to gain.

Saying that six weeks before a stage announcement was the real decision on this project. I had to carry product, engineering and the CEO's office with me, so I did not argue it as a design preference. I argued it as exposure: a chat box moves the burden of knowing what to ask onto the seller, most sellers do not know what to ask, and a launch sellers politely ignore is worse than no launch at all.

What I put in its place was one sentence.

The seller stops operating and starts reviewing. Manual work becomes a conversation, and repeated conversations become automations. The reframe I wrote in week two and held the project to

A direction is only worth writing if it settles arguments without its author in the room. This one decided what belonged in chat and what belonged on the panel, required a rupee figure on every recommendation, and ruled out silent execution, which made refusals easy to explain and hard to argue with.

The five rules underneath it

One moment sits under all three surfaces: the seller stops doing something and lets the system do it. That cannot be redesigned case by case by whoever builds the next agent, so I wrote it as a standard, applying on any surface, in any module, including agents that did not exist yet.

  • One thread, many agents

    Separate agents with separate scopes and permissions. The seller sees one conversation and never has to work out which agent picked up.

  • Problems come to the seller

    No hunting. If NDR spikes or the wallet runs low, it appears on the screen where it can be acted on, with the fix attached.

  • State the scope in the seller's units

    Not "shipping your orders" but "15 COD orders via Ekart, ₹1,474, five-day delivery". Sellers approve quantities and rupees, so confirmations use those terms.

  • Give the reason, never a confidence score

    "Highest COD delivery rate on this route" is something a seller can evaluate. A percentage certainty is a number they have no way to check, offered in place of one they could.

  • Separate reading from doing

    An agent may answer and explain freely. Anything that spends money, ships a parcel or creates a standing rule takes an explicit press. Fluency never leaks into execution.

The reason to fix this before the second agent rather than after the tenth is arithmetic. A mistake in a form costs one seller one order. The same mistake in a confirmation pattern, replicated across modules and running unattended, costs thousands of sellers at once.

Surface one

Chat, with a dedicated agent per module

Chat sits beside the panel rather than on top of it, so the seller watches the order list update as the conversation acts on it. That keeps the panel as the source of truth and makes the agent's work verifiable at a glance, instead of asking sellers to trust a summary.

Each module has its own agent with its own data scope. Separation keeps answers grounded, contains failures to one domain, and let us ship one agent at a time. Orders, Dashboard and Help and Support are live; the architecture takes more.

The empty state does real work: instead of a blinking cursor, the three questions sellers actually ask first, drawn from the query corpus, so the first interaction succeeds rather than teaching the seller what this thing can do.

A call I had to make

How much reasoning to show, and when

The tension: sellers said they needed evidence to trust the system, but a wall of reasoning on every reply makes a fast tool feel slow.

What I did: the answer leads; the reasoning is one click behind it on every response. Nothing is summarised away, and nothing is forced on a seller who has already decided to trust that answer type.

What I watch: if expansion rates stay high after weeks of use, the answers are not carrying enough evidence on their own and the disclosure is compensating for it.

Surface two

Insights that carry their own fix

Insight cards sit across the panel, and each has exactly three parts: a number, a root cause, and an action. That was a constraint I set for the whole system, if we could not state the cause and offer a next step, it was a metric, not an insight, and it did not earn a card.

A card alone is not enough. "Delivery is two days faster this month" invites a follow-up, so every card opens into chat where the seller can ask which courier moved and pull the trend. The card is the entry point; the conversation is the diagnosis.

It is also where the handoff happens. The seller taps an insight, the insights agent explains it, the seller says take action, and the orders agent executes, one thread, no visible seam. Designing that seam away was most of the work: consistent voice, consistent card grammar, and no moment where the interface admits it has changed hands.

Surface three

My Agents, automation described in a sentence

Sellers write the rule they want in plain language. The system parses it into a trigger, a condition and an action, and renders it as a workflow the seller reads before saving. No drag and drop, no configuration screens, but no black box either, because the parsed logic is handed straight back for confirmation.

The preview was the hardest thing on the project to get right. Early versions confirmed the rule conversationally and testers could not tell what they had agreed to. Showing the branch, including the false path, is what made people willing to press save. An automation a seller cannot picture is one they will never trust with 500 orders.

A call I had to make

Plain language in, structured logic out

The tension: natural language is how sellers think about their rules. It is also ambiguous, and an ambiguous rule running unattended across hundreds of orders is expensive.

What I did: accept the sentence, never execute the sentence. The parsed structure is always shown back as the thing being saved, so the seller confirms logic rather than phrasing.

The tradeoff: it adds a step to a flow whose whole promise was speed. I took that cost knowingly, an automation is a standing decision, and standing decisions deserve a confirmation.

The other half

The panel underneath the agents

Not every module becomes a conversation. Settings, returns, finance screens and the dense order tables are still panel UI, and they always will be, some work is faster to do by looking at a grid than by describing it. Which meant the revamp had two halves, and the unglamorous half decided whether the whole thing held together.

If the agentic surfaces were new and the rest of the panel was untouched, sellers would feel the seam every time they left a conversation. So I redesigned the panel in the same system: a new information architecture and menu structure, a settings hierarchy rebuilt around what sellers actually change, and a screen-by-screen pass over the data tables deciding which columns earn their place at a glance and which belong behind a detail view.

One shell, two modes. Agentic modules add a chat panel and insight cards to the same frame; non-agentic modules keep the frame and simply get better. A seller cannot tell where the revamp ends and the agentic layer begins, which is the entire point.

Why the theme changed

The short answer: colour had to stop being decoration and start carrying meaning, and there was none left to spend.

The old panel used Shiprocket's brand colour across chrome, buttons, links and highlights. That is a perfectly good choice for a product where the seller makes every decision, brand presence costs nothing when nothing on screen needs to be distinguished from anything else. It stops working the moment the system starts making claims. An agentic panel has to signal three different things at a glance: what the system found on its own, what it is proposing to do with your money, and what the seller has already decided. If the brand colour is already on every button and every heading, there is no colour left to say "this came from the system, and it is waiting on you".

So I demoted brand from surface treatment to accent and made the working canvas neutral. One hue, a deep green, is reserved exclusively for agentic surfaces: the insight panel, the chat, the agent preview. It is never used for anything a seller did themselves. Three reasons for that particular green rather than a brighter accent:

  • It stays out of the alert palette

    Red and amber belong to NDR spikes, RTO risk and wallet warnings. An AI surface that borrowed those would make every proactive insight read as an emergency, when most of them are opportunities.

  • It does not collide with the couriers

    Courier brand colours appear throughout the data, in recommendations, comparisons and performance views. The system's own colour has to sit apart from the things it is comparing, or the interface looks like it is taking sides.

  • It holds up over a long session

    Sellers are on this screen for hours. A saturated accent applied to a persistent panel becomes fatiguing quickly. A deep, low-chroma green stays legible and calm beside dense tables, and gives the pale insight cards inside it enough contrast to lead.

The result is a panel where colour answers a question instead of decorating an answer: neutral is your workspace, green is the system speaking, red is something wrong. Brand still shows up, in the logo, the accents, the tone, but it no longer occupies the vocabulary the product needs for meaning.

Limits

Designing what the system says it cannot do

Once a parcel is with a courier, the platform can diagnose an NDR or an RTO but cannot reattempt, reroute or recall it. The easiest thing for a conversational product to do is blur that boundary, an agent that has been helpful for ten minutes will happily sound helpful for one more.

I made the opposite call. Asked about failed deliveries already in the courier network, the system explains exactly what is happening, which day, which courier, which delivery window, which pin codes, and then stops, because the honest next step is prevention rather than rescue.

It costs a moment of disappointment and buys the thing the product depends on. A system that overstates its reach once will be checked forever afterwards, and a seller who has to verify everything has not saved any time at all.

How it was made

Six weeks, in the order I ran them

Nothing about the timeline was comfortable, so the sequencing had to be strict: no pixel work before the problem was framed, and no expansion before one flow worked end to end.

  1. Listening before deciding

    Week 1

    The four research streams, run in parallel.

  2. Framing, and turning down the obvious answer

    Week 1–2

    The reframe and the five rules, taken to product, engineering and the CEO's office and agreed as the arbitration standard for everything that followed. The highest-leverage week of the project, and the one that produced no screens.

  3. System before screens

    Week 2–3

    Tokens and the new colour system, the insight card, the chat surface, the thought-process disclosure, the agent preview, and the language patterns for how an agent asks, proposes, confirms and refuses. In a product that generates responses nobody individually designs, the copy patterns are load-bearing: without them every agent invents its own voice and the illusion of one assistant collapses.

  4. One flow all the way through

    Week 3–4

    Orders first, because that is where sellers spend their morning. Chat, insight, handoff, execution, confirmation, finished before touching another module, so the interaction problems were found once rather than three times.

  5. Breaking it on purpose

    Week 4–5

    UAT and adversarial testing, run by me: ambiguous instructions, wrong pin codes, contradictory rules, out-of-scope requests. Several of the rules above came from watching an agent answer confidently and wrongly.

  6. Live in front of 500 sellers

    Week 6

    SHIVIR. Stage announcement, then two days of booth demos with fixes going in between sessions.

How I used AI to design an AI product

Not a story about generating screens faster. It changed which parts of six weeks I spent my own hours on.

Where the tools did the work

Production, synthesis, volume

Flows. Around 70% of screens generated in Claude Design against my design system, which made variations cheap enough to compare rather than argue about.

Synthesis. The query corpus was larger than I could read. Clustering it surfaced what sellers actually ask, which set agent scope and the empty-state prompts.

Pressure-testing. Generating adversarial inputs, bad phrasings, contradictory rules, out-of-scope demands, to throw at our own agents during UAT.

Where I did the work

Judgement, and the hard 30%

The reframe. No tool produces the decision to turn down the version everyone was expecting.

The hard 30%. Dense states, edge cases, the agent preview, and every screen engineering needed pinned down precisely, refined in Figma.

The rules. Written after watching a confident, wrong answer in testing and deciding what the interface owes a seller in that moment.

The summary: AI compressed production, and I spent every hour it returned on research, testing and judgement. Being a heavy user of agentic tools while designing one was itself research, the places those tools lost my trust are the places I designed against in ours.

Launch

Sixteen minutes on stage, then two days at the booth

The demo had to make one argument to a room that had heard AI promises before: this is not a dashboard that shows you numbers, it is a system that tells you what they mean and then acts. I structured it as a single unbroken thread rather than a feature tour, the product's claim is that you never leave the conversation, so the demo could not leave it either.

A new seller onboarded in five minutes. An established seller's panel opened with three findings nobody had asked for: eight orders stuck before pickup, an NDR spike in one city, a delivery improvement. The stuck orders were diagnosed and fixed in chat using the seller's own shipment history, and the repeated fix became an automation, built by describing it. Then the NDR question, answered with the day, the courier and the delivery window causing it, and an honest limit on what could still be done about orders already dispatched. Two screen transitions in sixteen minutes.

Then two days at the booth, in front of more than 500 sellers using it cold while I watched. It is the closest thing to unmoderated testing at scale you get in a day, and the fastest correction to any assumption I was still carrying.

  • Almost nobody typed first

    Given a chat box, sellers hesitated. Given an insight card with a number on it, they engaged immediately and asked their questions from there, the clearest confirmation that leading with findings was right.

  • The rupee figure was the moment of belief

    Reactions changed visibly when a recommendation named an amount. Sellers stopped evaluating the interface and started evaluating the decision, the shift the product needs them to make.

  • The sceptics wanted the automations

    Sellers unconvinced by chat lit up when they could describe a rule and watch it become a workflow. Automation was legible to them in a way conversation was not, which now shapes how I think the product should be sequenced by segment.

  • Everyone asked what happens when it is wrong

    Early and repeatedly, the first question of someone doing the arithmetic on handing over their operations, and a vindication of putting the preview and confirm patterns in before beta rather than after.

Fixes went in between sessions: clearer scope language on bulk confirmations, better empty-state prompts. The booth also gave us a recruitment pool, the beta cohort came from sellers who tried it there and asked for access, survey respondents, and existing sellers chosen for spread across order volume and category, so it was not made up entirely of enthusiasts.

Measurement

What we agreed to judge it on, before we shipped

An agentic product invites vanity metrics, messages sent, questions answered, sessions opened. None tell you whether a seller's work got better, so I pushed for the frame to be agreed before launch, and not on whichever number happened to look good afterwards.

Reading the beta

The 65 sellers who went back are the most useful thing we have

A third of the cohort returned to the old panel, and the post-beta interviews showed the pattern the research had already warned me about: high-volume sellers with trained staff and years of muscle memory, for whom a conversation is slower than a keyboard path used ten thousand times. Speed and familiarity are not the same thing, and I underweighted the second.

The read is not that the model is wrong but that it was delivered in the wrong order for that segment. For a seller running 2,000 orders a day, the insight layer and My Agents are the valuable parts and chat is optional. For a seller running 50, chat is the whole product. That is now the shape of the next release: the same system, sequenced by segment rather than shipped uniformly.

In flight

The decisions this cycle

The beta is still running, so these are live questions rather than conclusions. Each has a decision attached that I expect to make before this goes wider.

  • Is trust growing, or is disclosure propping it up?

    Tracking how often sellers expand Thought Process over time. A falling rate means confidence is building. A flat one means the answers are not carrying enough evidence, and the fix belongs in the response design rather than the disclosure.

  • What does the transition from the old panel look like?

    The switch-back rate is a migration problem more than a product one. I am designing a guided path, the agentic surfaces introduced inside the panel a seller already knows, rather than a destination they must commit to.

  • How should an agent behave when it is unsure?

    I tested this adversarially, but sellers mostly saw the happy path before beta. Uncertainty is core to an agentic experience, not an edge case, and deserves its own research round with real accounts and real money at stake.

  • Which agent comes next, and on what evidence?

    Chosen from where sellers are still doing manual work inside the agentic panel, not from what is easiest to build. The measurement frame above exists to answer exactly this.

Durability

What outlasts the project

Six weeks produces a beta. What I was trying to leave behind was a set of things the company can build on without me in the room: a direction other teams use to settle their own scope questions, a design system that covers language as well as components, agent rules written to be inherited by agents nobody has built yet, a working method for AI-native design proven under a real deadline, and a research loop that decides the roadmap on evidence.

Reflection

What I took from leading this

The hardest part was not the interaction design. It was holding a direction against a deadline, an expectation and a stage date, when the cheaper answer was available and would have shipped comfortably. Most of the value I added in six weeks was created in week two, in a decision that produced nothing to show.

The second is where design sits in agentic products. These systems are a negotiation over control, how much a person hands over, on what evidence, with what way back. That negotiation is not owned by the model or by engineering; it happens in the interface, in the confirmations, the disclosures and the refusals. Whoever writes those rules decides how much the company can safely automate. On this project I made sure that was a designer.
