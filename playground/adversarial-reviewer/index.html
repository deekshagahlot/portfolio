USING A MODEL TO FIND WHAT ISN’T THERE

Reviewing my own work with an adversarial prompt

OVERVIEW

Instead of asking a model to generate a design, I asked it to attack one. I gave it a flow I’d already signed off and asked what states, cases, and failure paths were missing — then triaged the list myself.

THE PROBLEM

You can’t review a screen for what isn’t on it

Design review looks at the canvas. The expensive mistakes are the things that never reached the canvas at all — the empty state nobody drew, the partial-failure case, the permission variant, what happens on a slow connection. Those don’t get caught by looking harder at the frame. They get caught by someone holding a checklist of everything a flow could need and going down it, which is tedious and therefore skipped.

I wanted to know whether a model was good at exactly that tedium.

APPROACH

Ask for absence, not output

I described the flow state by state: entry points, happy path, what data each screen depends on, what the user can do at each step. Then I asked for what was missing, specifically: empty states, error and partial-failure states, loading and latency behaviour, permission and role variants, edge cases in the data, and accessibility gaps.

Then I triaged. Every item got sorted into real gap, deliberate omission, or doesn’t apply here. The triage is the design work — the list is just raw material.

LEARNING
Models are better at absence than at presence

Generating a design means making a hundred judgement calls about a specific context. Listing what a flow of this type usually needs is recall against a pattern, and that’s where the model is strong. It found eleven things I’d missed on a flow I’d already signed off. High recall is the point: I’d rather dismiss five irrelevant items than miss one real one.

HICCUP
Low precision, and it can’t tell you what matters

Roughly seven of the items didn’t apply — states that don’t exist in this product, cases ruled out by an earlier decision it had no way of knowing about. It also flagged everything at the same severity. A missing empty state and a missing offline mode arrived as equal-weight bullets. Deciding which gaps were worth the build cost still needed someone who knew the roadmap, the users, and what we’d already chosen not to support.

OUTCOME

A better checklist, not a better reviewer

This is now a standing step before I hand anything off. It doesn’t replace review — it front-loads the boring half so review can be about judgement instead of recall. The honest framing: the model produced the list, I produced the decisions, and the decisions were the part that took the skill.
